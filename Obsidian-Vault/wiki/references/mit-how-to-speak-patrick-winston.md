# MIT How to Speak — Patrick Winston

> [!tldr] A technical talk is a designed path for helping an audience value and accept an idea: promise the payoff, give the idea landmarks, show the work, and leave the audience with contributions.

## Source and Capture

## Core Heuristics

- **Empowerment promise:** tell the audience what it will be able to know or do by the end. ^[extracted]
- **Cycling:** return to important ideas because attention temporarily drops for some listeners. ^[extracted]
- **Fence:** distinguish an idea from superficially similar ideas so the audience understands its boundary. ^[extracted]
- **Verbal punctuation:** announce sections and transitions so listeners can re-enter the talk after drifting. ^[extracted]
- **Questions:** ask questions that are neither embarrassing because they are obvious nor impossible because they are too hard; Winston describes roughly seven seconds of waiting as a practical upper bound. ^[extracted]

These heuristics form a low-tech attention protocol: orient the audience, mark the structure, and make recovery from distraction cheap. ^[inferred]

## Boards, Props, and Slides

Winston argues that a board is useful for informing because writing speed is close to the speed at which an audience can absorb an idea. A board also gives the speaker a physical target and lets the audience follow a construction rather than receive a finished image. Props make abstract reasoning tangible; he speculates that this works partly through empathetic mirroring, which he presents as a personal view rather than an established result. ^[extracted]

His slide rules are deliberately restrictive:

- Do not read the slide; the audience already knows how to read. ^[extracted]
- Remove background clutter, logos, unnecessary titles, and excess words. ^[extracted]
- Leave enough white space and use a readable font size rather than shrinking text to fit. ^[extracted]
- Keep the speaker and slide in the same attention field; avoid turning away to operate a laser pointer. ^[extracted]
- Use arrows or other static cues when the audience needs help locating something. ^[extracted]

This complements [[concepts/anti-powerpoint|Anti-PowerPoint]]: content should lead the visual treatment, but the visual treatment must still preserve the speaker's channel. ^[inferred]

## Informing, Persuading, and Being Remembered

For an informing talk, Winston connects inspiration to three observable behaviors: the speaker shows passion, helps the audience see a problem in a new way, and gives an intelligible promise about the payoff. He describes thinking as story work: provide the stories, questions, mechanisms, composition methods, and reliability tests needed to reason. ^[extracted]

For oral exams and job talks, he recommends situating the work in its field and practicing with people who do not already understand it. A supervisor may fill in missing context automatically; naive listeners expose what the presentation actually communicates. ^[extracted]

He gives a five-minute test for a faculty job talk: establish a problem worth caring about and show that something has been done. The “done” side can be made concrete by enumerating the steps required to realize the vision and identifying which steps have been implemented. ^[extracted]

His “Winston Star” memory aid for making work recognizable consists of a symbol, slogan, salient idea, and story of how the work was done. ^[extracted]

## Ending Well

Winston recommends keeping collaborators on the first slide rather than spending the final slide on a long collaborator list. The final slide should state **contributions** and remain useful while questions are asked. ^[extracted]

He rejects a mandatory final “thank you” as a weak convention because it frames the audience as having endured the talk out of politeness. Alternative endings include a joke, a benediction, or a direct salute to the audience and host. ^[extracted]
