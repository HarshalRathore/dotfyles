# Technical Presentation

A **technical presentation** is not merely information placed on slides. It is a designed path that helps an audience understand, value, and accept an idea. Patrick Winston's [[references/mit-how-to-speak-patrick-winston|How to Speak]] supplies a practical repertoire for building that path. ^[extracted]

## Audience Path

1. **Promise the payoff.** State what the audience will know or be able to do by the end. ^[extracted]
2. **Give recovery landmarks.** Cycle important ideas, announce sections, and use verbal punctuation so a distracted listener can re-enter. ^[extracted]
3. **Fence the idea.** Explain how the contribution differs from nearby work or an apparently similar idea. ^[extracted]
4. **Show the work.** For a technical or job talk, situate the problem and enumerate what has been built or demonstrated. ^[extracted]
5. **Leave contributions visible.** Keep the final slide useful during questions and state what the work contributes instead of ending on a generic “conclusions” card. ^[extracted]

This is an attention-and-evidence protocol: the promise gives purpose, landmarks reduce navigation cost, and the contribution slide makes evaluation concrete. ^[inferred]

## Medium Rules

- Use a board or live construction when the audience benefits from seeing an idea unfold at absorption speed. ^[extracted]
- Use props when a physical object can make a relationship or mechanism memorable. ^[extracted]
- Use slides to expose or support ideas, not to replace the speaker's explanation. ^[extracted]
- Remove words, logos, background clutter, and unnecessary titles; preserve whitespace and readable type. ^[extracted]
- Use static arrows or labels instead of turning away to operate a laser pointer. ^[extracted]

This complements [[concepts/anti-powerpoint|Anti-PowerPoint]]: content should lead visual form, while presentation design should protect the audience's attention channel. ^[inferred]

## Practice and Persuasion

Practice with people who do not already know the work. An expert supervisor may unconsciously supply missing context; an uninformed listener reveals what the talk actually communicates. ^[extracted]

A job talk should quickly establish both a problem worth caring about and evidence that something has been done. Winston's five-minute test is a useful forcing function for technical presenters. ^[extracted]
